01

Why SRA decides the deal Foundation

Every solar project's revenue model sits on one chain: sunlight → energy → revenue → debt. The energy yield assessment (usually run in PVsyst) takes a weather file as its single biggest input. If that file is 4% too optimistic, the plant under-delivers by roughly 4% every year for 25 years, and no amount of good engineering recovers it.

Step 1Irradiance dataset (GHI, DHI, temperature) for the site
Step 2Transposition to the module plane (GTI / POA)
Step 3PV simulation: losses, temperature, clipping, availability
Step 4P50 energy and uncertainty → P75 / P90 / P99
Step 5Financial model: tariff × P90 → DSCR → debt sizing

In a TDD the developer (or seller) has already done steps 1–4. Our job is to answer three questions as an independent engineer:

Is the source right?

Was a credible, recent, validated dataset used — or the one that happened to give the highest number?

Is the number right?

Do independent sources and real measured data near the site agree within an acceptable band?

Is the uncertainty right?

Is the P90 built on a defensible uncertainty, or has uncertainty been understated to inflate P90?

Lender's lens. Banks size debt on P90 (sometimes one-year P99) energy. Two projects with the same P50 can carry very different debt if one has a 5% resource uncertainty and the other 8%. That is why "which dataset" and "what uncertainty" get argued so hard.
02

Sunlight 101: GHI, DNI, DHI, GTI Foundation

Sunlight reaching the ground arrives in two ways: straight from the sun's disc (beam / direct) and scattered by the sky, clouds and aerosols (diffuse). Every dataset describes these components.

TermMeaning
GHIGlobal Horizontal Irradiance — everything falling on a flat horizontal surface. The headline number every database publishes.
DNIDirect Normal Irradiance — beam light on a surface always facing the sun. Key for trackers and CSP.
DHIDiffuse Horizontal Irradiance — scattered sky light on a horizontal surface.
GTI / POAGlobal Tilted Irradiance / Plane-of-Array — what actually reaches the tilted or tracking modules. This drives energy.
GHI = DNI · cos(zenith) + DHI

Units that confuse people

  • Irradiance is power: W/m² (instantaneous, e.g. 1000 W/m² at noon).
  • Irradiation is energy: kWh/m² over a day, month or year. Datasets report annual GHI in kWh/m²/yr.
  • India's utility belts typically see roughly 1,800–2,100 kWh/m²/yr GHI; western Rajasthan and Kutch at the upper end, monsoon-heavy and coastal sites lower.
  • kWh/m²/day ÷ 1 kW/m² = "peak sun hours". 5.6 kWh/m²/day ≈ 2,045 kWh/m²/yr.

Why diffuse share matters

Two sites with identical GHI can produce different energy. A site with more diffuse light (haze, monsoon cloud) gains less from tilting and much less from tracking. That is why reviewers check DHI, not just GHI.

03

Where the data comes from Foundation

There are only four ways to know how much sun a site receives. Every database name you hear is one of these, or a blend.

① Ground measurement

Pyranometers on a met mast or inside an operating plant. The most accurate for that exact spot if the sensor is high class, cleaned, calibrated and quality-controlled. Limitations: usually only 1–3 years long, and only valid at that location.

Examples: on-site met station, plant SCADA pyranometers, NIWE SRRA stations, IMD radiation stations.

② Satellite-derived models

Geostationary weather satellites photograph cloud cover every 10–30 minutes. A model converts "cloud brightness" into ground irradiance, using clear-sky physics plus aerosol and water-vapour inputs. Gives 15–25+ years of continuous time series anywhere.

Examples: Solargis, NSRDB (NREL), CM SAF SARAH (used by PVGIS), SolarAnywhere, Vaisala.

③ Interpolation of stations

Long-term monthly averages from thousands of weather stations, interpolated to the site and blended with satellite where stations are sparse. Hourly values are then synthetically generated from those monthly means.

Example: Meteonorm.

④ Reanalysis

Global weather-model output corrected with observations (ECMWF ERA5, NASA MERRA-2). Excellent for temperature, wind and long-term trends; coarse (~25–50 km) and weaker on clouds for irradiance.

Examples: ERA5, MERRA-2, NASA POWER (built on these products).

Why India is a hard place for satellite models

Aerosols

Dust over Rajasthan and Gujarat in pre-monsoon, pollution and haze over the Indo-Gangetic plain, crop-burning smoke. Aerosols cut beam light and can't be seen well by the satellite, so the model depends on aerosol inputs (CAMS or climatology). This is the biggest single error source in India.

Monsoon & fog

June–September cloud regimes and north-Indian winter fog are highly variable year to year, raising inter-annual variability and making "typical year" choices tricky.

Satellite geometry

India is viewed by the Meteosat Indian-Ocean satellite; the eastern states are seen at a slant, which degrades pixel quality. Fewer high-quality public ground stations also mean fewer validation points.

What paid data buys you The databases

Free and paid datasets both start from satellites or stations. The accuracy difference comes from what goes into the model and how hard the provider works to validate it. These are the levers:

1. Better aerosol inputs

Using daily, near-real aerosol data (e.g. CAMS) rather than a monthly climatology. In India, where dust and haze swing day to day, this is the largest accuracy lever.

2. Higher time & space resolution

15-minute (or finer) values and fine spatial grids downscaled with terrain. Sub-hourly data matters for inverter clipping at high DC/AC ratios and for BESS sizing — hourly data hides short peaks.

3. Long, recent, continuous record

A real time series from the late 1990s or 2000s to last month captures true year-to-year variability and recent climate (aerosol trends). Free products often stop years ago.

4. Validation against ground stations

Providers that compare against hundreds of quality-controlled stations know their bias by region and climate, and can publish a site-specific uncertainty instead of a generic one.

5. Cloud, snow & terrain handling

Distinguishing bright desert or salt-pan surfaces from cloud (relevant for Rann of Kutch-type sites), horizon shading, elevation correction.

6. Bankability & licence

A recognised name, documented method and a licence that allows sharing with lenders. Part of what you pay for is that the lender's engineer will not re-open the question.

What paid data does NOT fix: it is still a model. In regions with high and changing aerosols — India is explicitly cited by providers as one — even the best commercial model can carry a systematic bias of several percent at a given site. That is exactly why ground measurements and our nearby-plant benchmark remain valuable.

06

TMY vs time series The databases

Time series

The actual recorded (modelled) weather, year by year: e.g. 2005, 2006 … 2024, at 15-min or hourly steps. You can simulate every year separately and see how energy really varies.

TMY — Typical Meteorological Year

One synthetic 8,760-hour year built by stitching together the most "typical" months from the time series (Jan from 2011, Feb from 2017, …). Statistically close to the long-term average. Fast and convenient for PVsyst — but by design it removes extremes.

P50 TMY

Represents the long-term median year. Used for the P50 energy simulation.

P90 TMY

A year constructed to sit at the 90% exceedance level of irradiance. Convenient, but it only reflects resource uncertainty, not losses or modelling.

Synthetic TMY (Meteonorm)

Generated from monthly averages using a stochastic model, not picked from real months. Useful, but it carries no real year-to-year information.

Known pitfall in South Asia: NREL's study of South Asia found that the energy from a TMY was frequently biased high relative to the true median year, and that no single calendar year represents P50 or P90 across the region. A TMY result should be cross-checked against simulating each year of the time series.

07

Uncertainty, P50 & P90 Advanced

P50 is the energy you expect to exceed half the time. P90 is the energy you expect to exceed in 9 years out of 10. The gap between them is set entirely by the combined uncertainty, which is built from independent components added in quadrature (root-sum-square).

Resource-side components

  • Long-term resource (model) uncertainty — how wrong the dataset's long-term average could be at this site. Largest term; reduced by site adaptation.
  • Inter-annual variability (IAV) — how much one year differs from another. For N years, it shrinks by √N.
  • Transposition / POA — GHI → GTI conversion, diffuse split, albedo.

Plant-side components

  • PV simulation model — module, temperature, mismatch, IAM modelling.
  • Soiling & losses — especially material in dusty Indian sites.
  • Degradation & availability — long-term assumptions.
Reading provider uncertainty. Providers often quote uncertainty at a confidence level (e.g. "±5% at 80%" or "P90 uncertainty ±6%"). The tool below needs the standard deviation (1σ). For a two-sided 80% interval, σ ≈ value ÷ 1.28; for 90%, σ ≈ value ÷ 1.645; for 95%, σ ≈ value ÷ 1.96.

What else to compare beyond the annual number

  • Monthly profile — a source can match annually but be wrong in monsoon months; this matters for seasonal PPA and BESS cases.
  • Diffuse fraction — differences of a few points change tracker and bifacial gains.
  • Data period — 1996–2015 vs 2005–2024 is not a like-for-like comparison.
  • Temperature — drives thermal losses; a 1–2 °C bias moves energy by roughly 0.4–0.8%.
  • Time step — hourly vs 15-min affects clipping estimates at DC/AC above ~1.3.

Our View: the nearby-plant benchmark Advanced

This is the additional, independent opinion we add on top of the database comparison. If we operate or monitor a plant within about 50–100 km of the target site, that plant's quality-controlled pyranometer data is real measured sunlight in the same climate. Comparing it against what the databases predicted for that operating plant's location tells us how biased the databases are in this region — and we can carry that correction to the target site.

Why this is a differentiator. A satellite provider validates against a few public stations. A large O&M portfolio spread across 150+ Indian sites is, in effect, a proprietary ground-truth network covering exactly the regions where projects are being bought and sold. Few TDD consultants can bring that.

Method, step by step

1 · SelectOperating plant(s) within ~50–100 km, same climate zone (coastal vs inland, similar elevation, similar aerosol regime)
2 · QC the sensorSensor class, calibration certificate, cleaning log, tilt/orientation, data gaps, night offsets, comparison between redundant sensors
3 · Pull the databaseSame database, same period, at the reference plant's coordinates (and same plane if comparing GTI)
4 · RatioMeasured ÷ database, monthly and annual. Check consistency across months and years
5 · TransferApply the bias to the target site's database value, weighted by confidence
6 · OpineState "Our View" GHI/GTI and how it compares with the developer's assumption

Pitfalls that ruin a nearby-plant comparison

Dirty or drifting sensors

An uncleaned pyranometer reads low and makes the database look optimistic. Check cleaning logs, compare morning-after-cleaning values, check redundant sensors and year-on-year drift.

Reference cells vs pyranometers

Silicon reference cells respond differently to spectrum and angle than thermopile pyranometers; raw values can differ by a few percent. Convert or treat with lower confidence.

Plane mismatch

POA sensors on a tracker or a 20° tilt can't be compared with a database's GHI. Transpose the database to the exact same geometry, or prefer a horizontal sensor.

Period mismatch

Compare measured 2023–2024 with database 2023–2024, never with the database's long-term average. Weather years differ by several percent.

Different climate pocket

50 km in India can cross from coastal to inland, or into a hill or industrial haze zone. Distance alone is not similarity.

Time stamps & gaps

IST vs UTC, interval-start vs interval-end labelling and filled gaps can create apparent biases. Align time stamps and compare only on common valid intervals.

Red flags

  • Developer's source is the highest of all sources by more than ~3%
  • Free or screening-grade dataset used as the sole basis
  • Synthetic hourly data used for P90 or clipping
  • Data period ending many years ago
  • Resource uncertainty below ~3% without site adaptation evidence
  • P90 computed from a P90 TMY alone, ignoring plant-side uncertainty
  • "Site measurement" shorter than a year, or with no QC or cleaning record
  • GTI gain inconsistent with diffuse fraction and mounting
